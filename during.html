During Your Experience

Navigating Your Active Project

Master the art of adaptive project management, human-centered fieldwork, and constructive feedback as you execute your project in real time.

The active phase of an engaged learning experience is where much of the real growth happens. Requirements may shift, team dynamics may evolve, client constraints may emerge, and research may reveal information that changes your understanding of the problem.

Use this phase to build resilience, adaptability, and ethical agility while continuing to work collaboratively with your partner.

Why the Active Phase Matters

Real-world projects rarely follow a perfect plan. As your team begins working, you may discover new information, encounter unexpected constraints, or receive feedback that changes your original direction.

These challenges are part of engaged learning. Rather than treating change as a failure, use it as an opportunity to learn more about the project, your partner, your team, and your own approach to problem-solving.

The goal is not simply to complete your original plan. Your team should continually evaluate whether your work is still aligned with partner needs and the goals of the project.

Phase 3: Managing & Executing in the Field

During the experience, your team moves from initial planning into active project work. This phase includes conducting research, tracking progress, maintaining communication with project sponsors, and responding to feedback.

Continue to balance project goals with the needs and perspectives of the people and organizations involved. Human-centered work requires attention to context, relationships, and the experiences of others.

What to Focus On

Fieldwork & Research Methods

Use human-centered design and research tools to observe contexts, understand experiences, and extract meaningful insights from your fieldwork.

Pay attention to what people do, how environments shape behavior, and what you learn through observation and interaction.

Sponsor & Client Updates

Maintain regular communication with project sponsors and clients. Share progress, identify challenges, communicate changes, and make sure the partner understands where the project stands.

Feedback Loops

Gather, evaluate, and integrate constructive feedback throughout the project rather than waiting until the very end.

Feedback can help your team identify problems, test assumptions, and make better decisions as the project develops.

Be Ready to Adapt

Your initial project plan is a starting point, not a guarantee that everything will happen exactly as expected. Engaged learning projects can change as your team learns more.

Changes you may encounter include:

  • A partner's priorities may change.
  • Your team may discover new information through research.
  • A technical approach may not work as expected.
  • Project timelines may change.
  • Team responsibilities may need to be adjusted.
  • New constraints may affect what can realistically be completed.
  • Feedback may require your team to reconsider an earlier decision.

When changes occur, return to the project's goals and communicate with your partner before making major decisions.

Manage Scope and Changing Needs

Scope creep occurs when additional work or expectations are added to a project without a corresponding adjustment to time, resources, or priorities.

Not every new request is inappropriate. Some changes may reveal that the original plan did not fully address the partner's needs. The important thing is to discuss changes openly and determine what is realistic.

When the project changes, ask:

  • What has changed?
  • Why is the change important?
  • How does it affect the original project goals?
  • How much additional time or effort would it require?
  • What would need to be removed or changed to make room for the new request?
  • Does the team and partner agree on the revised priorities?

Conduct Human-Centered Fieldwork

Fieldwork gives your team an opportunity to better understand the people, environments, activities, and interactions connected to your project.

Instead of focusing only on what you expect to find, remain open to observations that challenge your initial assumptions.

During fieldwork, consider:

  • What activities are taking place?
  • What environments shape the experience?
  • What interactions are important?
  • What objects or tools are being used?
  • Who is involved?
  • What patterns or unexpected behaviors are you noticing?
  • What questions do your observations raise?

Keep Your Partner Updated

Regular project updates help partners understand what your team is doing and provide opportunities to identify problems early.

Do not wait until the project is finished to communicate important changes or concerns. Transparent communication can make it easier to adjust plans together.

A useful project update can include:

  • What your team completed since the last update.
  • What your team is currently working on.
  • Important findings or insights.
  • Challenges or barriers.
  • Decisions that need partner input.
  • Upcoming milestones and deadlines.
  • Any changes to project scope or priorities.

Create Feedback Loops

Feedback is most useful when it becomes part of an ongoing process rather than a final evaluation.

Ask for feedback at meaningful points in the project, listen carefully to the response, and determine how the feedback should influence your next steps.

A simple feedback cycle:

  1. Ask. Identify what you need feedback on.
  2. Listen. Give your partner, teammates, or other stakeholders space to explain their perspective.
  3. Evaluate. Consider what the feedback means for your project goals and assumptions.
  4. Integrate. Decide what changes should be made.
  5. Communicate. Explain important decisions and next steps.

Practice Ethical Engagement

Challenges during an engaged learning project are not only technical. Your team may encounter ethical questions about research, communication, decision-making, community impact, or the way project outcomes are used.

When difficult situations arise, slow down and consider who may be affected by your decisions. Return to the project's goals, listen to the people involved, and communicate openly about concerns.

Ask yourself:

  • Who could be affected by this decision?
  • Whose perspective might be missing?
  • Are we making assumptions?
  • Have we communicated clearly with our partner?
  • Does this decision support a respectful and mutually beneficial partnership?

Navigate Team Challenges

Team dynamics can change as the project becomes more demanding. Different working styles, responsibilities, deadlines, and opinions can create challenges.

Address problems early rather than allowing frustration to build. Clear communication about responsibilities and expectations can help your team remain focused on the project's goals.

  • Discuss problems openly and respectfully.
  • Clarify responsibilities when work is unclear.
  • Make space for different perspectives.
  • Revisit deadlines and priorities when circumstances change.
  • Ask for support when your team needs it.

Mid-Project Check-In

Take a moment to evaluate how your project is progressing.

  • Are we still aligned with the project's goals?
  • Does our partner understand our current progress?
  • Have we discovered anything that changes our understanding of the problem?
  • Are our current priorities realistic?
  • Are team responsibilities clear?
  • Have we incorporated useful feedback?
  • Are there ethical concerns we need to address?
  • What should we change before moving forward?

Preparing to Wrap Up

As your project approaches completion, begin thinking about more than simply finishing the final deliverable. Consider how your work will be transitioned to your partner, how you will evaluate your impact, and what you learned from the experience.

A responsible ending starts before the final deadline. Begin organizing project materials, documenting important decisions, and discussing next steps with your partner.
